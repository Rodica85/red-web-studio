A site or app that "works on my machine" isn't the same as one that works for every real customer, on every real device, doing things in an order the developer didn't anticipate. That gap is where lost sales, support tickets, and one-star reviews usually come from. Testing exists to find that gap before your customers do, and report it clearly enough that it actually gets fixed.

Why Manual Testing Still Matters

Automated tests are good at repeating the same check thousands of times without getting bored. They're much worse at noticing that a button is technically clickable but confusingly placed, that an error message is accurate but unreadable, or that a checkout flow works but feels stressful. That's the gap manual testing fills: a real person, following real user behaviour, noticing the things a script was never told to look for. For most independent businesses, that combination catches far more of the problems that actually cost you customers than automation alone would.

What A Good Bug Report Looks Like

"The checkout is broken" isn't useful to a developer trying to fix it. A proper bug report includes exactly what was clicked, in what order, on what browser and device, what was expected to happen, and what actually happened, ideally with a screenshot or screen recording attached. That level of detail is the difference between a bug getting fixed in one pass and a developer spending an hour trying to reproduce something they can't see. It's a habit built over three years of freelance testing work, and it's the actual deliverable you're paying for, not just a list of complaints.
